# How To Invest Well

A brief summary of our investment framework.

If you consistently buy things for less than they're worth, one day you'll run out of things to buy. The trouble is it's not always clear what something is worth. Generally, we believe fund managers should be judged by their approach to managing that uncertainty.

## Managing Uncertainty Well

I can be pretty sure that a standard slice of decent pizza is a good deal at $1. I'm less sure of that if the cost is $5. At $10, I may choose to take my business elsewhere.

Similarly, every asset — real estate, stocks, art — has a true worth that is not knowable, but that we can guess at. Our view is that an asset's intrinsic value remains relatively constant over time. As a result, the price we're willing to pay should also remain constant. If we're lucky, market fluctuations can occassionally lead to an asset selling well below our estimate of fair value, making it attractive for purchase. All we need to do is **1)** be patient; and, **2)** be ready. See **Figure 1** below.

![A market-price curve oscillating around three flat reference levels: true worth, a lower estimated worth, and a still-lower acceptable purchase price.](images/content/market-price-value-levels.svg "Figure 1 || Volatile market prices swing around asset fair value, creating the potential for attractive opportunities to purchase.")

The chart above shows the ideal profile of an investment opportunity for us at Twin Cape, where we have:

1. A relatively conservative estimate of fair value;

2. An asset which experiences substantial market-price volatility; and,

3. An asset which occasionally trades within range of an acceptable purchase price.

We manage uncertainty by searching exhaustively for assets which satisfy all three of these criteria — in short, an asset that **1)** we understand; **2)** occassionally sells at low prices; and, **3)** occassionally sells at low enough prices for us to buy and earn a satisfactory return. Interestingly, though unsurprisingly, these represent both the vast majority of top contributors to our returns and a resounding minority of opportunities we encounter.

In the following sections, we present archetypes for assets we encounter more commonly, as measured by these three dimensions. We then explain the significance of the target return variable. Finally, we fit other, well-known investment approaches into this view of markets, for comparison purposes.

## Common Asset Archetypes

Unlike many investors, we perform relatively rigorous valuation analyses across our entire investable universe. Over time, we have noticed that these assets fall into three distinct categories: **1)** those that we cannot understand; **2)** those that essentially never trade at attractive prices; and, **3)** those that essentially always trade at unreasonably high prices, even after a substantial drawdown.

### Opaque Assets

Some assets cannot be valued. Rarely, it is because the methods of accounting cannot be relied upon. Most commonly, it is because we're a few professional designations short of being able to understand what the asset is.

These assets represent approximately 10% of the assets we have assessed to date, and we avoid them entirely. See **Figure 2** below.

![A market-price curve oscillating around true worth, with an unknown estimated worth and acceptable purchase price.](images/content/value_levels_estimated_and_price_unknown.svg "Figure 2 || Although every asset has a true worth, the information available to us may be insufficient to estimate that value.")

### Consensus Assets

Some assets — though potentially strong — rarely sell at prices that deviate from what we believe to be fair value. No matter the strength of the business, we think overpaying would be a mistake.

These assets represents approximately 8% of the assets we have assessed to date, and we pray they may be made available at lower prices. See **Figure 3** below.

![A market-price curve oscillating around true worth, with insufficient volatility to reach the estimated worth and acceptable purchase price.](images/content/value_levels_price_tracks_true_worth.svg "Figure 3 || Some assets may not be purchasable at attractive enough prices because their market values experience little volatility.")

### Overvalued Assets

Some assets may experience large swings in market price, but never reach the prices we would find attractive for purchase. Though there can be large returns made here as market sentiment fluctuates, we prefer to look elsewhere, as the losses can also be extravagent.

These assets represent approximately 80% of the assets we have assessed to date. While we ignore them for the most part, one or two of these high flyers may fall from grace from time to time, and we would not be opposed to buying at attractive prices when that occurs.

![A market-price curve oscillating far above true worth, estimated worth, and acceptable purchase price.](images/content/value_levels_below_market_trough.svg "Figure 4 || Though they often show high percentage gains, these assets trade far above their intrinsic value and may show high percentage losses with similar likelihood.")

## The Target Return

If Mark Zuckerberg woke up tomorrow and decided to sell me his entire stake in Meta for $1, it would be irresponsible for me to decline. In fact, I probably should not turn it down even if it cost me $100,000 (and you could add a few more zero's to boot). But, $100 trillion would likely be an overpay.

Every business is cheap at one price and expensive at another price. I think if you look at a company like Meta and say you'd pay no more than $100 billion for the entire thing, and then you mark 200 companies at similarly silly prices, you'll find five or six that are selling for less than what you'd pay and two or three that are selling for *far* less than what you'd pay.

If you would pay no more than $100 billion for Meta, then perhaps you would like to sell the company when it exceeds $100 billion in value. It follows then, that to realize a 20% return on investment, you would need to buy the company for no more than $83 billion. A 50% return would require a purchase price of no more than $67 billion.

The return you target defines your positive selection universe — the group of assets that trade at low enough prices for you to buy. If you require a lower rate of return, then you increase the number of assets you would consider purchasing. Similarly, if you require a very high rate of return, there may only be one or two assets selling cheaply enough to meet your needs.
